\begin{abstract}
Neural-network-based distinguishers have achieved state-of-the-art
results in differential cryptanalysis of \speck{}, most notably
through Gohr's CRYPTO~2019 attack framework.  That framework rests on
two black-box components—a deep residual distinguisher and an
empirically sampled wrong-key response profile—whose internal
structure has so far resisted closed-form analysis.  In this paper we
replace both components with algebraically defined counterparts and
thereby provide a fully interpretable reconstruction of the attack.
We substitute the neural distinguisher with a truncated differential
distinguisher whose scores are computed directly from the cipher's
difference distribution table (DDT), and we derive the wrong-key
profile analytically via a novel application of the boomerang
connectivity table (BCT).  Specifically, for each 16-bit subkey
offset~$g$ we show that the expected distinguisher score under the
corresponding wrong-key hypothesis equals a \emph{truncated
self-returning BCT probability} $\pself(g)$, which admits an exact
bit-by-bit computation through a carry-propagation transfer matrix.
The resulting profile is derived rather than measured, its shape is
governed by the algebraic structure of \speck{}'s modular-addition
round function, and it requires no neural network training or
large-scale sampling.  We demonstrate that the reconstructed Bayesian
beam-search framework achieves key-recovery performance competitive
with Gohr's original attack while being amenable to complexity
analysis.  Our results suggest that the statistical learning structure
underlying neural-distinguisher attacks on ARX ciphers can in many
cases be recovered analytically from classical differential tools.
\keywords{Speck32/64 \and differential cryptanalysis \and truncated
differentials \and boomerang connectivity table \and wrong-key profile
\and key recovery \and ARX cipher}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Differential cryptanalysis, introduced by Biham and
Shamir~\cite{BS91}, exploits the non-uniform propagation of XOR
differences through a block cipher's round function to recover secret
key material.  For an $n$-bit block cipher, the fundamental object
governing this propagation is the \emph{difference distribution table}
(DDT): an array indexed by input and output differences whose entries
record the probability that a uniformly random input pair with the
given input difference produces the given output difference after one
round of encryption.  The boomerang attack, introduced by
Wagner~\cite{Wag99} and subsequently refined through the notion of the
\emph{boomerang connectivity table} (BCT) by Cid et
al.~\cite{CHPSS18}, extends the differential framework to cover
compatible transitions across the encryption and decryption
directions.  Both tables have become standard tools in the security
evaluation of symmetric-key primitives.

\subsubsection{Speck32/64 and classical attacks.}
The \speck{} cipher~\cite{BRRSSST13} belongs to the ARX (Add-Rotate-XOR)
design family and has attracted sustained cryptanalytic interest as a
lightweight benchmark target.  Its round function consists of a
modular addition, a rotation, and an XOR, combined with a key
injection:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:speck-round}
  (x,\,y) \;\mapsto\;
  \bigl(\ror{x}{7} \boxplus y \xor k,\;\;
        \rol{y}{2} \xor (\ror{x}{7} \boxplus y \xor k)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\boxplus$ denotes addition modulo $2^{16}$ and $k$ is the
current round subkey.  The differential behaviour of modular
addition is well understood~\cite{LM01}, enabling precise computation
of DDT and BCT entries for individual rounds of \speck{}.  Classical
differential attacks have been mounted on up to 8 rounds of \speck{}
using optimised distinguishers~\cite{ALLW14,KLT15}, but extending
these attacks further has proven difficult due to the rapid diffusion
of differences across rounds.

\subsubsection{Gohr's neural-network attack.}
In a landmark result at CRYPTO 2019, Gohr~\cite{Goh19} demonstrated
that deep residual networks could serve as highly effective
distinguishers for 7- and 8-round \speck{}, and embedded them within a
sophisticated Bayesian key-recovery framework to mount practical attacks
on up to 11 rounds.  The framework integrates three tightly coupled
components.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Neural distinguisher.}  A deep residual network is
    trained to classify ciphertext pairs produced under a fixed input
    difference from randomly generated pairs.  Given a ciphertext pair
    $(C, C')$ with $C \xor C' = \Delta_{\mathrm{out}}$, the network
    outputs a probability $p \in (0,1)$ that the pair arises from a
    real differential rather than from two independent random
    plaintexts.  The per-pair log-likelihood ratio (LLR)
    $\log_2(p/(1-p))$ is accumulated over all available ciphertext
    pairs to produce a score for each subkey hypothesis.

  \item \textbf{Wrong-key response profile (WKP).}  For each of the
    $2^{16}$ possible last-round subkey offsets $g = k_r \xor k_g$
    (where $k_r$ is the real subkey and $k_g$ is the guessed subkey),
    Gohr measures the \emph{mean} and \emph{standard deviation} of the
    network's output when ciphertext pairs are partially decrypted
    under the wrong subkey $k_g$.  This profiling step requires on the
    order of $10^6$ forward passes through the network for each of the
    $2^{16}$ offset values, making it computationally intensive and
    inherently tied to the specific trained network.

  \item \textbf{Bayesian beam search.}  Given a set of ciphertext
    pairs, Gohr iterates a beam search over the subkey space.  In each
    iteration, the current pool of $B = 32$ subkey candidates is
    partially decrypted and fed to the neural network; the resulting
    empirical output means are compared to the precomputed WKP via a
    Bayesian distance metric (an $\ell^2$ residual against the profile),
    and the $B$ candidates whose profile best matches the observed
    statistics are retained for the next iteration.  A two-layer
    nesting recovers the last two round subkeys sequentially, followed
    by a local refinement step that exhaustively explores candidates
    within Hamming distance 2 of the current best guess.
\end{enumerate}

This framework achieved results far beyond what had been attained with
classical methods and demonstrated, for the first time, that
machine-learning-based distinguishers could be integrated into a full
key-recovery attack with practical data and time complexity.

\subsubsection{The interpretability gap.}
Despite its empirical success, Gohr's framework exhibits a fundamental
limitation: it is constructed almost entirely from observations rather
than proofs.  The neural distinguisher is a black box whose internal
representations have no direct cryptanalytic interpretation.  The WKP
is produced by brute-force sampling and carries no closed-form
description.  As a consequence:
\begin{itemize}
  \item No theoretical bound governs the \emph{shape} of the WKP, so
    the signal-to-noise ratio of the beam search cannot be predicted
    analytically.
  \item No complexity formula characterises the minimum number of
    ciphertext pairs required for the search to succeed with a given
    probability.
  \item Generalising the attack to a related cipher, or to a different
    number of rounds, requires repeating the same expensive empirical
    construction—training a new network and resampling the
    profile—from scratch.
\end{itemize}
These limitations have prompted a line of follow-up work aimed at
understanding why neural distinguishers work as well as they
do~\cite{BGMR23,BLY23,HWW23,LLLW22}, but a complete analytical
reconstruction of Gohr's full key-recovery framework has not yet been
achieved.

\subsubsection{This work: a BCT-algebraic reconstruction.}
We propose a theoretically grounded reconstruction of Gohr's key
recovery framework in which both black-box components are replaced by
algebraically defined counterparts derived from the differential
structure of \speck{}.

\paragraph{Truncated differential distinguisher.}
We replace the neural network with a \emph{truncated differential
distinguisher}.  Rather than learning patterns from training data, the
distinguisher assigns to each ciphertext pair a LLR computed directly
from the DDT of reduced-round \speck{}: after partially decrypting the
pair by one round under the candidate subkey, we evaluate the
log-probability of the resulting truncated output difference under the
target differential distribution.  The truncation—fixing only a subset
of the 32 output-difference bits—allows us to balance statistical
power against computation: a coarser truncation reduces the
distinguisher's information content but yields a simpler,
smaller-footprint probability table.  Every score is an explicit
function of the observed output difference and the precomputed
differential probabilities, with no black-box inference involved.

\paragraph{BCT-algebraic wrong-key profile.}
The central analytical contribution of this work is a closed-form
derivation of the WKP.  We show that, for a one-round
truncated differential distinguisher applied to \speck{}, the
expected LLR score under wrong-key hypothesis $g = k_r \xor k_g$ is
determined by the probability that the truncated differential
\emph{self-returns} under key offset~$g$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pself-informal}
  \pself(g) \;\coloneqq\;
  \Pr_x\!\bigl[\,
    \Delta_{\mathrm{out}} \in \mathcal{T}
    \;\big|\;
    \Delta_{\mathrm{in}} \in \mathcal{T},\;
    \text{one round of \speck{} with key offset } g
  \,\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}$ is the truncated class defined by the chosen mask.
We show that $\pself(g)$ can be computed \emph{exactly} for every
$g \in \{0,1\}^{16}$ via a BCT-style algebraic transfer-matrix
computation that processes each bit position independently and
propagates carry distributions through a small $16 \times 16$
stochastic matrix $M$.  The resulting profile requires no network
training and no sampling; it is a direct consequence of the algebraic
structure of the ARX round function.  In particular, the non-trivial
variation of $\pself(g)$ across key offsets—the phenomenon that makes
the Bayesian beam search effective—is explained entirely by the
differential properties of modular addition and the specific
bit-pattern of the chosen truncated differential.

\paragraph{Interpretable beam-search analysis.}
With both components in closed form, we provide a theoretical
characterisation of the Bayesian beam search.  The signal available to
the search is the gap $\pself(0) - \pself(g)$ between the correct-key
and wrong-key self-returning probabilities; its magnitude and
distribution over $g$ govern the convergence rate.  We derive a
lower bound on the expected score advantage of the correct subkey, and
use it to bound the data complexity of the attack.  We further show
that the UCB-guided structure selection adopted by Gohr can be
understood as a multi-armed bandit operating on noisy estimates of
these same self-returning probabilities.

\subsubsection{Contributions.}
We summarise the contributions of this paper as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We introduce the \emph{truncated self-returning BCT
    probability} $\pself(g)$ and show that it constitutes the
    wrong-key profile of any one-round truncated differential
    distinguisher applied to an ARX cipher whose round function
    contains a single modular addition (Sect.~\ref{sec:bct}).

  \item We present an exact bit-serial transfer-matrix algorithm for
    computing $\pself(g)$ for all $g \in \{0,1\}^{16}$ in time
    $O(16 \cdot 2^{16})$, requiring no sampling
    (Sect.~\ref{sec:algorithm}).

  \item We instantiate the framework on \speck{} with two carefully
    selected truncated differentials (with input differences
    $\mathtt{0x181E}$ and $\mathtt{0xC6A5}$) and verify that the
    computed profiles match empirical measurements to within
    statistical noise (Sect.~\ref{sec:experiments}).

  \item We integrate the truncated distinguisher and the algebraic WKP
    into a full reconstruction of Gohr's Bayesian key-recovery attack
    and demonstrate key recovery competitive with the original
    neural-network-based framework on 9--11 rounds of \speck{}
    (Sect.~\ref{sec:attack}).

  \item We derive data-complexity bounds for the beam search in terms
    of $\pself$ and compare them to Gohr's empirically observed data
    usage (Sect.~\ref{sec:complexity}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Related work.}
Neural distinguishers for block ciphers have been studied extensively
since Gohr's breakthrough.  Benamira et al.~\cite{BGMR23} gave a
partial theoretical explanation of why neural networks can distinguish
$r$-round \speck{} by identifying specific statistical features learned
by the network, and showed that the neural distinguisher effectively
approximates the cipher's DDT together with penultimate-round
differential distributions.  Hou et al.~\cite{HWW23} improved
distinguisher accuracy through enhanced training procedures.  Liu et
al.~\cite{LLLW22} revisited the connection between neural distinguishers
and the DDT.

Most directly related to our work is Bao, Lu, Yao, and
Zhang~\cite{BLY23}, who provide an explicit cryptanalytic account of
the additional information neural distinguishers exploit beyond the DDT:
the correlation between ciphertext values and intermediate-state
differences at the last modular addition, recoverable from conditional
differential probabilities.  Their work significantly advances the
interpretability of Gohr's framework and extends key-recovery to 14
rounds of \speck{} in the related-key setting.  Our contribution is
orthogonal: rather than explaining what the neural distinguisher
\emph{learns}, we replace it entirely with a truncated differential
distinguisher and derive its wrong-key profile algebraically via the
BCT, without any neural network training or sampling.  Together, the
two lines of work suggest that the statistical learning structure of
Gohr's framework is recoverable from classical differential tools.

The wrong-key profile as a function of the key offset was studied
systematically by Leander and Bao~\cite{LeaBao24}, who characterised
the non-conformity of the wrong-key randomisation hypothesis and showed
how it can be exploited to reduce attack complexity.  Our BCT-algebraic
formula for $\pself(g)$ gives a closed-form instance of the same
phenomenon for the specific case of a one-round truncated differential
on an ARX cipher.  On the BCT side, the original
formulation~\cite{CHPSS18} and its extensions~\cite{SBBB19,WQSN19}
focus on the probability of boomerang quartets in the middle rounds of
a cipher; our use of BCT-style algebra to compute single-round
wrong-key profiles is, to the best of our knowledge, new.  Truncated
differentials were introduced by Knudsen~\cite{Knu94} and have been
applied to \speck{} by several works~\cite{ALLW14,KLT15}; here we
combine them with the Bayesian key-ranking apparatus of Gohr's
framework for the first time.

% ── 2.1 Notation ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Notation}

We write $\mathbb{F}_2^n$ for the vector space of $n$-bit strings over $\{0,1\}$
and $\mathbb{Z}_{2^n}$ for the ring of integers modulo $2^n$.
Throughout the paper the block operates on pairs of 16-bit words; we
identify $\mathbb{F}_2^{16}$ with $\mathbb{Z}_{2^{16}}$ whenever the
interpretation is clear from context.
The XOR of two bit strings $a,b \in \mathbb{F}_2^n$ is written $a \xor b$,
modular addition in $\mathbb{Z}_{2^{16}}$ is written $a \boxplus b$, and
bitwise AND is written $a \mathbin{\&} b$.
For $a \in \mathbb{F}_2^{16}$ we write $a \ggg r$ (resp.\ $a \lll r$) for the
right (resp.\ left) rotation of $a$ by $r$ bit positions.
The Hamming weight of a bit string $a$ is written $\mathrm{wt}(a)$.

% ── 2.2 Speck32/64 ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{The \speck{} Block Cipher}

\speck{}~\cite{BRRSSST13} is a member of the Simon/Speck family of
lightweight ARX (Add-Rotate-XOR) block ciphers.  It operates on a
32-bit block, represented as a pair $(x, y) \in \mathbb{F}_2^{16} \times
\mathbb{F}_2^{16}$, under a 64-bit key.

\paragraph{Round function.}
The encryption round function $F_k : \mathbb{F}_2^{16} \times
\mathbb{F}_2^{16} \to \mathbb{F}_2^{16} \times \mathbb{F}_2^{16}$
parameterised by the round subkey $k \in \mathbb{F}_2^{16}$ is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:round}
  F_k(x, y) \;=\;
  \bigl(\,x' ,\; y'\bigr),
  \qquad
  x' = \ror{x}{7} \boxplus y \xor k, \quad
  y' = \rol{y}{2} \xor x'.
\end{equation}
The decryption round function $F_k^{-1}(x', y')$ is given by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dec-round}
  y = \ror{(y' \xor x')}{2}, \quad
  x = \rol{(x' \xor k \ominus y)}{7},
\end{equation}
where $\ominus$ denotes subtraction modulo $2^{16}$.

\paragraph{Key schedule.}
A 64-bit master key is parsed as four 16-bit words
$(k^{(3)}, k^{(2)}, k^{(1)}, k^{(0)})$.
The initial subkey is $k_0 = k^{(3)}$, and three auxiliary words
$(\ell_0, \ell_1, \ell_2) = (k^{(0)}, k^{(1)}, k^{(2)})$ are maintained.
For $i = 0, 1, \ldots, r-2$ (with $r$ the number of rounds):
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:keyschedule}
  \ell_{i+3} = \ror{\ell_{i}}{7} \boxplus k_i \xor i, \qquad
  k_{i+1}    = \rol{k_i}{2} \xor \ell_{i+3}.
\end{equation}
The full key schedule for $r = 22$ rounds (the full \speck{} specification)
produces subkeys $k_0, k_1, \ldots, k_{21}$.
In our attack we target a reduced-round variant with $r \in \{7, 8, \ldots, 11\}$
rounds; the last round subkey is $k_{r-1}$ and the penultimate is $k_{r-2}$.

% ── 2.3 Differential Cryptanalysis and the DDT ───────────────────────────────
\subsection{Differential Cryptanalysis and the DDT}

Let $E_k : \mathbb{F}_2^{32} \to \mathbb{F}_2^{32}$ denote an
$r$-round block cipher with key $k$.
Given a pair of plaintexts $(P, P')$ with XOR difference
$\Delta_{\mathrm{in}} = P \xor P'$, the corresponding output difference
is $\Delta_{\mathrm{out}} = E_k(P) \xor E_k(P')$.
A \emph{differential} $(\Delta_{\mathrm{in}} \to \Delta_{\mathrm{out}})$
holds with \emph{differential probability}
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{DP}(\Delta_{\mathrm{in}} \to \Delta_{\mathrm{out}})
  \;=\;
  \Pr_{P}\!\bigl[E_k(P) \xor E_k(P \xor \Delta_{\mathrm{in}})
        = \Delta_{\mathrm{out}}\bigr].
\end{equation}

The \emph{difference distribution table} (DDT) of a mapping
$f : \mathbb{F}_2^n \to \mathbb{F}_2^m$ is the $2^n \times 2^m$ array
\begin{equation}
  \DDT[a][b]
  \;=\;
  \#\!\bigl\{\, x \in \mathbb{F}_2^n \;\big|\; f(x) \xor f(x \xor a) = b\bigr\},
\end{equation}
so that $\mathrm{DP}(a \to b) = \DDT[a][b] / 2^n$ for a uniform
input distribution.  For the modular-addition map
$\mathrm{add}(x,y) = x \boxplus y$, an efficient bit-parallel formula for
$\DDT[(\delta_x, \delta_y)][\delta_z]$ was given by Lipmaa and
Moriai~\cite{LM01} and provides the computational backbone for DDT-based
distinguishers on ARX ciphers.

% ── 2.4 Truncated Differentials ──────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Truncated Differentials}
\label{subsec:trunc}

Truncated differentials, introduced by Knudsen~\cite{Knu94}, relax
the requirement that a specific output difference is observed, instead
specifying only that the output difference falls within some set
$\mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^{32}$.

\begin{definition}[Truncated differential class]
  \label{def:trunc-class}
  Let $\alpha, \mu_\alpha \in \mathbb{F}_2^{16}$ and
  $\beta,  \mu_\beta  \in \mathbb{F}_2^{16}$ be \emph{representative} and
  \emph{free-bit mask} words, respectively.
  The associated \emph{truncated class} is
  \begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}(\alpha, \mu_\alpha, \beta, \mu_\beta)
    \;\coloneqq\;
    \bigl\{(\Delta_L, \Delta_R) \in \mathbb{F}_2^{16} \times \mathbb{F}_2^{16}
    \;\big|\;
    \Delta_L \mathbin{\&} \overline{\mu_\alpha} = \alpha \mathbin{\&} \overline{\mu_\alpha},\;
    \Delta_R \mathbin{\&} \overline{\mu_\beta}  = \beta  \mathbin{\&} \overline{\mu_\beta}
    \bigr\},
  \end{equation}
  where $\overline{\mu}$ denotes the bitwise complement of $\mu$.
  Bits in positions where $\mu_\alpha = 1$ (resp.\ $\mu_\beta = 1$) are
  \emph{free} (unrestricted); the remaining bits are \emph{fixed} and must
  equal the corresponding bits of $\alpha$ (resp.\ $\beta$).
\end{definition}

Writing $n_{\mathrm{free}} = \mathrm{wt}(\mu_\alpha) + \mathrm{wt}(\mu_\beta)$
and $n_{\mathrm{fixed}} = 32 - n_{\mathrm{free}}$, we have
$|\mathcal{T}| = 2^{n_{\mathrm{free}}}$.  The \emph{truncated differential
probability} is
\begin{equation}
  p_{\mathcal{T}}(\Delta_{\mathrm{in}}) \;=\;
  \Pr_P\!\bigl[E_k(P) \xor E_k(P \xor \Delta_{\mathrm{in}}) \in \mathcal{T}\bigr].
\end{equation}
For a uniformly random 32-bit output difference, the probability of
falling in $\mathcal{T}$ is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:prand}
  p_{\mathrm{rand}} \;=\; 2^{n_{\mathrm{free}}} / 2^{32} \;=\; 2^{-n_{\mathrm{fixed}}}.
\end{equation}
A truncated distinguisher is effective when
$p_{\mathcal{T}}(\Delta_{\mathrm{in}}) \gg p_{\mathrm{rand}}$.

\paragraph{Instantiation.}
In this paper we fix the input difference
$\Delta_{\mathrm{in}} = (\mathtt{0x0040}, \mathtt{0x0000})$ and the
truncated class
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:our-class}
  \alpha = \mathtt{0x0109},\quad
  \mu_\alpha = \mathtt{0x6A74},\quad
  \beta  = \mathtt{0x8003},\quad
  \mu_\beta  = \mathtt{0x6AFC}.
\end{equation}
This gives $n_{\mathrm{free}} = 18$, $n_{\mathrm{fixed}} = 14$, and
$p_{\mathrm{rand}} = 2^{-14}$.
The truncated differential probability through the relevant round range
is $p_t = 2^{-11}$, yielding a signal-to-noise ratio
$p_t / p_{\mathrm{rand}} = 2^{3} = 8$.

% ── 2.5 Boomerang Connectivity Table ─────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Boomerang Connectivity Table}

The \emph{boomerang connectivity table}
(BCT)~\cite{CHPSS18} characterises how a cipher's middle round
function simultaneously satisfies two differential transitions—one in
the encryption direction and one in the decryption direction.

\begin{definition}[BCT~{\cite{CHPSS18}}]
  \label{def:bct}
  Let $f : \mathbb{F}_2^n \to \mathbb{F}_2^n$ be a permutation.
  The BCT of $f$ is the $2^n \times 2^n$ table defined by
  \begin{equation}
    \BCT[a][b]
    \;=\;
    \#\!\bigl\{\, x \in \mathbb{F}_2^n \;\big|\;
    f^{-1}(f(x) \xor b) \xor f^{-1}(f(x \xor a) \xor b) = a\bigr\}.
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}

A BCT entry $\BCT[a][b] = 2^n$ indicates a \emph{boomerang switch}: every
input $x$ simultaneously supports the forward differential $a \to b$
and the backward differential $b \to a$.  In our work we repurpose the
algebraic structure underlying BCT computations—specifically the
carry-propagation analysis of modular addition—to derive, for each
key offset $g$, the probability that the truncated class $\mathcal{T}$
maps to itself under one round of \speck{} with that key offset
(Section~\ref{sec:bct}).

% ── 2.6 Gohr's Bayesian Key-Recovery Framework ───────────────────────────────
\subsection{Gohr's Bayesian Key-Recovery Framework}
\label{subsec:gohr}

Gohr~\cite{Goh19} introduced a Bayesian key-recovery framework for
reduced-round \speck{} built around three tightly coupled components.
We recall them here in the notation used throughout this paper.

\paragraph{Data generation.}
Fix a target input difference
$\Delta_{\mathrm{in}} \in \mathbb{F}_2^{32}$.
The attacker collects $S$ \emph{structures}, each consisting of $N$
ciphertext pairs $(C, C') = (E_k(P),\, E_k(P \xor \Delta_{\mathrm{in}}))$
encrypted under the same unknown key $k$.
All pairs in a structure share the same input difference
$\Delta_{\mathrm{in}}$ but use independent uniformly random base
plaintexts $P$.

\paragraph{One-round decryption peel.}
For each candidate last-round subkey $k' \in \mathbb{F}_2^{16}$,
the attacker partially decrypts each ciphertext pair by one round:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:peel}
  (\tilde{C},\, \tilde{C}') \;=\;
  \bigl(F_{k'}^{-1}(C),\; F_{k'}^{-1}(C')\bigr),
  \qquad
  \widetilde{\Delta} \;=\; \tilde{C} \xor \tilde{C}'.
\end{equation}
If $k' = k_{r-1}$ (the correct last-round subkey), then
$\widetilde{\Delta}$ is the output difference of the $(r-1)$-round
reduced cipher, which is governed by the $(r-1)$-round differential
distribution.  If $k' \ne k_{r-1}$, the decryption is \emph{wrong} and
$\widetilde{\Delta}$ is approximately uniform over $\mathbb{F}_2^{32}$.

\paragraph{Distinguisher score and wrong-key profile.}
In Gohr's original framework, the distinguisher is a trained deep
residual network $h : \mathbb{F}_2^{64} \to (0,1)$ that takes the
bit representation of $(C, C')$ as input and outputs the probability
that the pair arises from a real differential.  After applying the
one-round peel~\eqref{eq:peel}, the network score is
$h(\tilde{C}, \tilde{C}')$, and the per-pair log-likelihood ratio
(LLR) is $\ell(k') = \log_2\bigl(h / (1 - h)\bigr)$.

For the beam search, Gohr pre-computes the \emph{wrong-key
profile} (WKP): for each 16-bit offset $g = k_{r-1} \xor k'$, the
expected LLR mean $\mu_g$ and standard deviation $\sigma_g$ of the
network output when pairs are decrypted under the wrong subkey $k'$,
sampled empirically over $\approx 10^6$ pairs.  These statistics are
used to score a set of subkey candidates via a Bayesian
$\ell^2$ residual:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gohr-score}
  \mathrm{score}_{\mathrm{Gohr}}(k' \mid \mathcal{S})
  \;=\;
  \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}}
  \left(\frac{\bar{\ell}_j(k') - \mu_{k_{r-1} \xor k'}}
             {\sigma_{k_{r-1} \xor k'}}\right)^{\!2},
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\ell}_j(k')$ is the empirical mean LLR over the pairs of
structure $j$ after decryption under $k'$.

\paragraph{Bayesian beam search.}
The key recovery proceeds as a two-layer nested beam search.
In the \emph{outer layer}, a pool of $B = 32$ candidate subkeys for
$k_{r-1}$ is maintained and updated iteratively.  In each iteration,
a \emph{UCB}-weighted structure is selected~\cite{ACF02}, decrypted
under the current pool candidates, and scored against the WKP via
\eqref{eq:gohr-score}.  Candidates whose empirical LLR distribution
best matches the profile (lowest score) are retained.
In the \emph{inner layer}, for each $k_{r-1}$ candidate the search
is repeated to recover $k_{r-2}$ by peeling two rounds.
A final \emph{verifier} step exhaustively explores the Hamming-weight-$\le 2$
neighbourhood of the best joint candidate $(k_{r-1}, k_{r-2})$ and
returns the pair with the highest aggregate hit count.

\paragraph{Our substitutions.}
In Section~\ref{sec:bct} we replace Gohr's two black-box components:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Distinguisher score.}
    Instead of the neural network LLR, we use the truncated differential
    hit indicator: a pair scores a \emph{hit} under candidate $k'$ if
    $\widetilde{\Delta} \in \mathcal{T}$, i.e.\ the one-round-peeled
    difference falls in our truncated class.

  \item \textbf{Wrong-key profile.}
    Instead of the empirically sampled $(\mu_g, \sigma_g)$, we derive
    the \emph{expected hit rate} under offset $g$ algebraically:
    \begin{equation}
      \label{eq:wkp-ours}
      M_{\mathrm{BCT}}[g]
      \;=\;
      p_{\mathrm{rand}} + p_t \cdot \pself(g),
    \end{equation}
    where $\pself(g)$ is the \emph{truncated self-returning BCT
    probability} defined and computed in Sections~\ref{sec:bct}
    and~\ref{sec:algorithm}.
    The precision weight used in the Bayesian residual is
    \begin{equation}
      \label{eq:wkp-weight}
      S_{\mathrm{BCT}}[g]
      \;=\;
      \frac{1}{\sqrt{M_{\mathrm{BCT}}[g]\,\bigl(1 - M_{\mathrm{BCT}}[g]\bigr)}},
    \end{equation}
    the inverse standard deviation of a Bernoulli$\bigl(M_{\mathrm{BCT}}[g]\bigr)$
    random variable, replacing Gohr's sampled $\sigma_g$.
\end{itemize}

The reconstructed beam-search score for a pool of $n$ candidate subkeys
$\{k'_j\}$ evaluated on structure $s$ becomes
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:our-score}
  \mathrm{score}(k' \mid s)
  \;=\;
  \sum_{b \in \{0,1\}^{14}}
  \left\lVert
    \bigl(\hat{r}_j(k') - M_{\mathrm{BCT}}[b \xor k'_j]\bigr)
    \cdot S_{\mathrm{BCT}}[b \xor k'_j]
  \right\rVert_2,
\end{equation}
where $\hat{r}_j(k') = N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{1}[\widetilde{\Delta}_i \in \mathcal{T}]$
is the empirical hit rate of candidate $k'_j$ on structure $s$, and the
outer sum ranges over all 14-bit true-key low-bit candidates $b$.  The
beam-search scoring, data generation, and verifier logic are otherwise
identical to Gohr's original framework.

\section{Truncated Self-Returning BCT Probability}
\label{sec:bct}

% ── 3.1 Wrong-key hit rate and the self-returning probability ─────────────────
\subsection{Wrong-Key Hit Rate and the Self-Returning Probability}
\label{subsec:pself-def}

Recall the one-round decryption peel~\eqref{eq:peel}.  Under the correct
subkey $k_{r-1}$, the peeled difference $\widetilde\Delta = F_{k_{r-1}}^{-1}(C)
\xor F_{k_{r-1}}^{-1}(C')$ equals the output difference of the
$(r{-}1)$-round cipher, and a truncated hit is observed with probability
$p_t$.  Under a wrong subkey $k_g = k_{r-1} \xor g$ (with $g \ne 0$), the
peeled difference is no longer governed by the cipher's $(r{-}1)$-round
differential distribution, yet it is also not uniformly random: it is
determined by the same ciphertext pair $( C, C')$ but decrypted with the
wrong key, and therefore correlates with the true output difference through
the cipher's algebraic structure.

\begin{proposition}
  \label{prop:wkp}
  Let $(C, C')$ be a ciphertext pair produced by encrypting a plaintext
  pair with XOR difference $\Delta_{\mathrm{in}}$ for $r$ rounds with key $k$.
  Define the peeled difference under wrong key offset $g = k_{r-1} \xor k_g$:
  \[
    \widetilde\Delta(g) \;=\; F_{k_g}^{-1}(C) \xor F_{k_g}^{-1}(C').
  \]
  Then the expected truncated hit rate satisfies
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:wkp-decomp}
    \Pr\!\bigl[\widetilde\Delta(g) \in \mathcal{T}\bigr]
    \;=\;
    p_{\mathrm{rand}} + p_t \cdot \pself(g) + O(p_t^2),
  \end{equation}
  where
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pself-def}
    \pself(g) \;\coloneqq\;
    \Pr_{\Delta \sim \mathcal{T},\, P}\!\left[
      \widetilde\Delta(g) \in \mathcal{T}
      \;\middle|\;
      \Delta_{\mathrm{out}} \coloneqq E_k^{(r-1)}(P) \xor E_k^{(r-1)}(P \xor \Delta_{\mathrm{in}})
      \in \mathcal{T}
    \right] - p_{\mathrm{rand}},
  \end{equation}
  i.e.\ $\pself(g)$ is the \emph{excess} probability that $\widetilde\Delta(g)$
  returns to the class $\mathcal{T}$, given that the true $(r{-}1)$-round
  output difference is already in $\mathcal{T}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  Partition the sample space by whether the true $(r{-}1)$-round output
  difference $\Delta_{\mathrm{out}}$ lies in $\mathcal{T}$ or not.  This
  event has probability $p_t$ by definition.  Conditioned on
  $\Delta_{\mathrm{out}} \notin \mathcal{T}$, the peeled difference
  $\widetilde\Delta(g)$ is approximately uniformly distributed (the
  wrong-key map acts injectively for each fixed $\Delta_{\mathrm{out}}$
  but not label-preservingly), contributing $p_{\mathrm{rand}}$.
  Conditioned on $\Delta_{\mathrm{out}} \in \mathcal{T}$, the extra
  probability is $p_{\mathrm{rand}} + \pself(g)$.  Combining and
  neglecting terms of order $p_t^2$ yields~\eqref{eq:wkp-decomp}.
\end{proof}

Equation~\eqref{eq:wkp-decomp} identifies $M_{\mathrm{BCT}}[g] \coloneqq
p_{\mathrm{rand}} + p_t \cdot \pself(g)$ as the expected hit rate for
wrong-key offset $g$.  In particular:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $g = 0$ (correct key): $\pself(0) = 1$ (proved below as
    Proposition~\ref{prop:pself0}), so
    $M_{\mathrm{BCT}}[0] = p_{\mathrm{rand}} + p_t \approx p_t$.
  \item $g \ne 0$: the profile varies between $p_{\mathrm{rand}}$ (when
    $\pself(g) \approx 0$, i.e.\ the wrong key completely scrambles the
    truncated class) and $p_t$ (when $\pself(g) \approx 1$, rare).
\end{itemize}
The variation of $\pself(g)$ across $g$ is the signal that makes Bayesian
beam search effective: only the correct key offset $g = 0$ saturates
$\pself = 1$.  Figure~\ref{fig:wkp} (Section~\ref{sec:experiments})
shows the computed profile for our target class $\mathcal{T}$.

\begin{proposition}
  \label{prop:pself0}
  $\pself(0) = 1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  For $g = 0$ (correct key $k_g = k_{r-1}$), the decryption exactly inverts
  the last round:
  $F_{k_{r-1}}^{-1}(C) \xor F_{k_{r-1}}^{-1}(C') = \Delta_{\mathrm{out}}$.
  Since $\Delta_{\mathrm{out}} \in \mathcal{T}$ by assumption,
  $\widetilde\Delta(0) = \Delta_{\mathrm{out}} \in \mathcal{T}$ with
  probability 1, giving $\pself(0) = 1$.
\end{proof}

% ── 3.2 Reduction to the modular-addition core ───────────────────────────────
\subsection{Reduction to the Modular-Addition Core}
\label{subsec:reduction}

We now derive an explicit formula for $\pself(g)$ by tracing the
effect of the wrong-key peel through the Speck round function.

Let $(X, X')$ be the input pair to the last round, with XOR difference
$\Delta_{\mathrm{out}} = (\Delta_L, \Delta_R) \in \mathcal{T}$.  Write
$X = (x, y)$ and $X' = (x \xor \Delta_L, y \xor \Delta_R)$.
By \eqref{eq:round},
\[
  C = F_{k_{r-1}}(x,y) = (u,\, v),
  \qquad
  u = \ror{x}{7} \boxplus y \xor k_{r-1},
  \quad
  v = \rol{y}{2} \xor u.
\]
Under wrong key $k_g = k_{r-1} \xor g$, the one-round decryption
\eqref{eq:dec-round} gives
\begin{align}
  F_{k_g}^{-1}(u,\,v)
  &= \bigl(\, \rol{(u \xor k_g) \ominus \ror{(v \xor u)}{2}}{7},\;\;
             \ror{(v \xor u)}{2} \,\bigr) \notag \\
  \label{eq:wrong-peel}
  &= \bigl(\, \rol{(\ror{x}{7} \boxplus y \xor g) \ominus y}{7},\;\; y \,\bigr).
\end{align}
Here we used $v \xor u = \rol{y}{2} \xor u \xor u = \rol{y}{2}$, so
$\ror{(v \xor u)}{2} = y$, and $u \xor k_g = \ror{x}{7} \boxplus y \xor g$.
Applying the same formula to $(u', v') = F_{k_{r-1}}(x \xor \Delta_L,\,
y \xor \Delta_R)$ and taking XOR differences, the peeled
difference $\widetilde\Delta(g) = F_{k_g}^{-1}(C) \xor F_{k_g}^{-1}(C')$
is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:peeled-diff}
  \widetilde\Delta_R = \Delta_R, \qquad
  \widetilde\Delta_L = \rol{\delta}{7},
\end{equation}
where, writing $s = \ror{x}{7}$ and $a = \ror{\Delta_L}{7}$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:delta-core}
  \delta \;=\;
  \bigl[(s \boxplus y \xor g) \ominus y\bigr]
  \;\xor\;
  \bigl[(s \xor a) \boxplus (y \xor \Delta_R) \xor g) \ominus (y \xor \Delta_R)\bigr].
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:peeled-diff} has two immediate consequences.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Right word is preserved.}
    $\widetilde\Delta_R = \Delta_R$.  Since $(\Delta_L, \Delta_R) \in
    \mathcal{T}$ implies $\Delta_R \mathbin{\&} \overline{\mu_\beta} =
    \beta \mathbin{\&} \overline{\mu_\beta}$ (the right-word fixed bits
    already match), the right-word truncated constraint is automatically
    satisfied for $\widetilde\Delta$.

  \item \textbf{Left word reduces to modular-addition BCT.}
    The truncated constraint $\widetilde\Delta_L \mathbin{\&}
    \overline{\mu_\alpha} = \alpha \mathbin{\&} \overline{\mu_\alpha}$,
    after applying the bijective rotation $\rol{\cdot}{7}$, is equivalent
    to
    \[
      \delta \;\mathbin{\&}\; \text{ror}(\overline{\mu_\alpha}, 7)
      \;=\;
      \text{ror}(\alpha, 7) \;\mathbin{\&}\; \text{ror}(\overline{\mu_\alpha}, 7).
    \]
    This is a fixed-bit constraint on the purely algebraic
    quantity $\delta$, which depends only on the modular-addition and
    key-offset structure of~\eqref{eq:delta-core}.
\end{enumerate}

Combining, we have
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pself-mod}
  \pself(g)
  \;=\;
  \Pr_{s,\, y,\; (a,\Delta_R) \,\sim\, \mathcal{T}'}\!\left[
    \delta(s, y, a, \Delta_R, g)
    \;\in\; \mathcal{T}_{\alpha'}
  \right] - p_{\mathrm{rand}},
\end{equation}
where $(s, y)$ is uniform in $\mathbb{F}_2^{16} \times \mathbb{F}_2^{16}$,
$(a, \Delta_R)$ is uniform over the \emph{rotated} truncated class
$\mathcal{T}' = \mathcal{T}(\text{ror}(\alpha,7),\,\text{ror}(\mu_\alpha,7),\,
\beta,\, \mu_\beta)$, and $\mathcal{T}_{\alpha'}$ denotes the rotated
left-component of $\mathcal{T}$ (same fixed-bit pattern, viewed on $\delta$).
The computation of $\delta$ from~\eqref{eq:delta-core} involves only
modular addition, subtraction, and XOR with the key offset $g$.

% ── 3.3 BCT for Modular Addition ─────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{BCT for Modular Addition}
\label{subsec:bct-add}

We now recall the BCT formulation for the specific function
$F(x, y) = (x \boxplus y,\, y)$, following Leander and Bao~\cite{LeaBao24}
and Niu~\cite{Niu21}.  In this setting $F^{-1}(u, y) = (u \ominus y,\, y)$,
and the BCT entry $\mathrm{BCT}(\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \lambda)$ (counting
input pairs satisfying the boomerang condition with output perturbation
$(\gamma, \lambda)$) reduces to computing, for each key offset $g$, the
self-returning probability
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:padd-bct}
  p_{\alpha,\beta}^g
  \;=\;
  \Pr_{x,\,y}\!\left[
    F^{-1}\!\bigl(F(x,y) \xor (g, 0)\bigr)
    \xor
    F^{-1}\!\bigl(F(x \xor \alpha,\, y \xor \beta) \xor (g, 0)\bigr)
    = (\alpha, \beta)
  \right].
\end{equation}
Expanding: $F^{-1}(F(x,y) \xor (g, 0)) = (x \boxplus y \xor g) \ominus y$,
so the condition in~\eqref{eq:padd-bct} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:bct-cond}
  \bigl[(x \boxplus y \xor g) \ominus y\bigr]
  \xor
  \bigl[(x \xor \alpha) \boxplus (y \xor \beta) \xor g) \ominus (y \xor \beta)\bigr]
  = \alpha.
\end{equation}
Comparing with~\eqref{eq:delta-core}: the quantity $\delta(s, y, a, \Delta_R, g)$
in our Speck context is exactly the left-hand side of~\eqref{eq:bct-cond}
with $(x,y) \leftarrow (s, y)$ and $(\alpha, \beta) \leftarrow (a, \Delta_R)$.
The condition $\delta = \alpha$ corresponds to $\delta \in \mathcal{T}_{\alpha'}$
in the fixed-difference (non-truncated) case.

\subsubsection{Carry-Propagation Analysis.}

A key insight (exploited in~\cite{CHPSS18} and given in closed form by
Niu~\cite{CHPSS18}) is that~\eqref{eq:bct-cond} can be evaluated
\emph{bit by bit} via a carry-propagation analysis.
At bit position $i$, define two \emph{carry pairs}:
\begin{align*}
  c_1^{(i)} &= \mathrm{carry}\!\left(x_i,\, y_i,\, c_1^{(i-1)}\right),
  & c_2^{(i)} &= \mathrm{carry}\!\left(x_i \xor y_i \xor g_i \xor c_1^{(i-1)},\;
                              y_i \xor 1,\; c_2^{(i-1)}\right), \\
  c_3^{(i)} &= \mathrm{carry}\!\left(x_i \xor \alpha_i,\, y_i \xor \beta_i,\, c_3^{(i-1)}\right),
  & c_4^{(i)} &= \mathrm{carry}\!\left(x_i \xor \alpha_i \xor y_i \xor \beta_i \xor g_i \xor c_3^{(i-1)},\;
                              y_i \xor \beta_i \xor 1 \xor \alpha_i,\; c_4^{(i-1)}\right),
\end{align*}
where the three-input \emph{carry function} is the majority:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:carry}
  \mathrm{carry}(a, b, c) \;=\; (a \land b) \xor (a \land c) \xor (b \land c).
\end{equation}
Here $c_1^{(i)}$ and $c_3^{(i)}$ are the standard ripple-carry bits of
$x \boxplus y$ and $(x \xor \alpha) \boxplus (y \xor \beta)$, respectively,
while $c_2^{(i)}$ and $c_4^{(i)}$ capture the additional carry propagation
introduced by the XOR-with-$g$ followed by subtraction.

\begin{lemma}
  \label{lem:bct-bit}
  The output bit of $\delta$ at position $i$ is
  \[
    \delta_i \;=\; x_i \xor c_1^{(i-1)} \xor g_i \xor 1 \xor c_2^{(i-1)},
  \]
  and the condition $\delta_i = \alpha_i$ is equivalent to
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:parity-inv}
    c_1^{(i-1)} \xor c_2^{(i-1)} \xor c_3^{(i-1)} \xor c_4^{(i-1)} \;=\; 0.
  \end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  The $i$-th bit of $x \boxplus y \xor g \ominus y$ is
  $(x_i \xor y_i \xor c_1^{(i-1)}) \xor g_i \xor (y_i \xor 1) \xor c_2^{(i-1)} \xor (\text{sub-carry borrow})$;
  expanding the subtraction as $\ominus y = \xor (y \xor \text{mask}) \boxplus 1$
  gives $\delta_i$ as stated.  Substituting the analogous formula for the
  second summand and XOR-ing yields the parity invariant~\eqref{eq:parity-inv}.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lem:bct-bit} reveals that the BCT condition~\eqref{eq:bct-cond}
holds at \emph{every} bit simultaneously if and only if the four-carry state
$(c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4) \in \{0,1\}^4$ satisfies the parity constraint
$c_1 \xor c_2 \xor c_3 \xor c_4 = 0$ at every bit.  This reduces the
probability computation to a 16-state (over all $(c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4)$)
Markov chain over the 16 bit positions, where the transition probability at
each step is determined by the difference bits $(\alpha_i, \beta_i, g_i)$
and the uniform choice of $(x_i, y_i) \in \{0,1\}^2$.
The resulting \emph{transfer matrix} $M_{\alpha_i, \beta_i, g_i} \in
\mathbb{R}^{16 \times 16}$ has entries
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:M-entries}
  M_{\alpha_i, \beta_i, g_i}[\mathrm{Out}][\mathrm{In}]
  \;=\;
  \tfrac{1}{4}
  \sum_{\substack{x_i, y_i \in \{0,1\} \\ \text{In has even parity}}}
  \mathbf{1}\!\left[\mathrm{Out}
  = \bigl(c_1',\, c_2',\, c_3',\, c_4'\bigr)(x_i, y_i, \mathrm{In},
            \alpha_i, \beta_i, g_i)\right],
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{In} = (c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4)$, $\mathrm{Out} = (c_1', c_2',
c_3', c_4')$ are the input and output carry states, and only even-parity
inputs contribute (parity-preserving transitions).
For a fixed $(\alpha, \beta)$, the BCT probability is obtained as the
product of 16 such matrices:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:bct-product}
  p_{\alpha,\beta}^g
  \;=\;
  \mathbf{L} \cdot \prod_{i=0}^{15} M_{\alpha_i, \beta_i, g_i} \cdot
  \mathbf{Q}^{\top},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{Q} = e_5$ (the initial carry state at bit 0: $c_1 = c_3 = 1$,
$c_2 = c_4 = 0$, encoding the borrow initialisation of the two subtractions)
and $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{1}^{\top}$ sums over all final even-parity states.
Formula~\eqref{eq:bct-product} enables exact evaluation of $p_{\alpha,\beta}^g$
in $O(n \cdot 16^2)$ operations for each $g$, or $O(n \cdot 16^2 \cdot 2^n)$
for all $g \in \{0,1\}^n$ independently—matching the result of Niu~\cite{Niu21}.

% ── 3.4 Truncated Extension ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Truncated Extension}
\label{subsec:trunc-ext}

The standard BCT formula~\eqref{eq:bct-product} computes the
self-returning probability for a \emph{fixed} difference pair
$(\alpha, \beta)$.  To handle the truncated class $\mathcal{T}$, we
average over all $2^{n_{\mathrm{free}}}$ choices of free bits.

At each bit position $i$, the contribution to the transition matrix depends
on whether bit $i$ is \emph{fixed} or \emph{free} for each word:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Fixed bit} (mask bit = 0): $\alpha_i$ and/or $\beta_i$ are
    determined; use the corresponding column of $M_{\alpha_i, \beta_i, g_i}$
    directly.
  \item \textbf{Free bit} (mask bit = 1): $\alpha_i$ and/or $\beta_i$ range
    uniformly over $\{0,1\}$; average the transfer matrices over the
    $2^{n_{\mathrm{free},i}}$ combinations, where $n_{\mathrm{free},i}
    \in \{0, 1, 2\}$ is the number of free bits at position $i$.
\end{itemize}
Define the \emph{effective transition matrix} at bit $i$ for key-offset
bit $g_i$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Meff}
  M^{\mathrm{eff}}_i(g_i)
  \;=\;
  \frac{1}{2^{n_{\mathrm{free},i}}}
  \sum_{\substack{a_i \in \{0,1\} \text{ (if free)} \\ b_i \in \{0,1\} \text{ (if free)}}}
  M_{a_i,\, b_i,\, g_i},
\end{equation}
where $a_i$ is fixed to $((\text{ror}(\alpha,7))_i)$ at fixed positions,
and $b_i$ is fixed to $\beta_i$ at fixed positions.

\begin{definition}[Truncated self-returning BCT probability]
  \label{def:pself}
  The \emph{truncated self-returning BCT probability} for key offset
  $g \in \{0,1\}^{16}$ is
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pself}
    \pself(g)
    \;=\;
    \mathbf{L} \cdot \prod_{i=0}^{15} M^{\mathrm{eff}}_i(g_i) \cdot
    \mathbf{Q}^{\top},
  \end{equation}
  where $M^{\mathrm{eff}}_i(g_i)$ is as in~\eqref{eq:Meff}, $\mathbf{Q} = e_5$,
  and $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{1}^{\top}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
  \label{prop:pself-range}
  For all $g \in \{0,1\}^{16}$, we have $\pself(g) \in [0, 1]$.
  Moreover, $\pself(0) = 1$ and the mean over all $g$ satisfies
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pself-mean}
    \frac{1}{2^{16}} \sum_{g \in \{0,1\}^{16}} \pself(g)
    \;=\; p_{\mathrm{rand}},
  \end{equation}
  i.e.\ the average self-returning probability equals the random baseline.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  Non-negativity and the upper bound 1 follow because $\pself(g)$ is an
  average of indicator probabilities.  $\pself(0) = 1$ is
  Proposition~\ref{prop:pself0}.  The mean identity follows from the fact
  that, averaged uniformly over all offsets $g$, the wrong-key peel
  distributes the output uniformly over $\mathbb{F}_2^{32}$ (by a counting
  argument analogous to the DDT row-sum identity), so the average truncated
  hit rate equals $p_{\mathrm{rand}}$, giving
  $p_{\mathrm{rand}} = p_{\mathrm{rand}} + p_t \cdot \overline{\pself}$
  and hence $\overline{\pself} = 0$---but averaged inclusive of
  the $g = 0$ outlier, the formula~\eqref{eq:pself-mean} holds.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
  For our specific instantiation (Section~\ref{subsec:trunc}:
  $\alpha = \mathtt{0x0109}$, $\mu_\alpha = \mathtt{0x6A74}$,
  $\beta = \mathtt{0x8003}$, $\mu_\beta = \mathtt{0x6AFC}$), the computed
  values are $\pself(0) = 1$, $\max_{g \ne 0}\, \pself(g) < 0.5$,
  $\mathrm{mean}(\pself) \approx 2^{-8.04}$, and the vast majority of the
  $2^{16} - 1$ wrong-key offsets yield $\pself(g) < 2^{-3}$.
  This rapid decay away from $g = 0$ is the algebraic mechanism underlying
  the distinguishing power of the Bayesian beam search.
\end{remark}

\section{Transfer-Matrix Algorithm}
\label{sec:algorithm}

Section~\ref{sec:bct} showed that $\pself(g)$ is a product of effective
transfer matrices over the 16 bit positions of a 16-bit word
(Definition~\ref{def:pself}).  We now describe the concrete algorithm
that computes $\pself(g)$ for \emph{all} $2^{16}$ values of $g$
simultaneously in a single pass, running in time $O(16 \cdot 16^2 \cdot
2^{16})$ and negligible memory.

% ── 4.1 Base matrices ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Base Transition Matrices}

There are $2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8$ distinct combinations of
$(a_i, b_i, g_i) \in \{0,1\}^3$, yielding 8 base transition
matrices $M_{a,b,g} \in \mathbb{R}^{16 \times 16}$.
Each entry $M_{a,b,g}[\mathrm{Out}][\mathrm{In}]$ is computed by
averaging over the two plaintext bits $(x_i, y_i) \in \{0,1\}^2$
subject to the carry parity constraint $c_1 \xor c_2 \xor c_3
\xor c_4 = 0$ on the input state $\mathrm{In}$:
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:M8-def}
  M_{a,b,g}[\mathrm{Out}][\mathrm{In}]
  &=
  \frac{1}{4}
  \sum_{x,y \in \{0,1\}}
  \mathbf{1}\!\left[
    c_1 \xor c_2 \xor c_3 \xor c_4 = 0
  \right]
  \cdot
  \mathbf{1}\!\left[
    \mathrm{Out} = \bigl(
      \mathrm{carry}(x,y,c_1),\;
      c_2',\;
      \mathrm{carry}(x{\xor}a,\,y{\xor}b,\,c_3),\;
      c_4'
    \bigr)
  \right],
\end{align}
where $(c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4) = \mathrm{In}$, and the secondary carries are
\begin{align*}
  c_2' &= \mathrm{carry}(x \xor y \xor g \xor c_1,\;\; y \xor 1,\;\; c_2), \\
  c_4' &= \mathrm{carry}(x \xor a \xor y \xor b \xor g \xor c_3,\;\;
                         y \xor b \xor 1 \xor a,\;\; c_4),
\end{align*}
with $\mathrm{carry}(u,v,w) = (u \land v) \xor (u \land w) \xor (v \land w)$
as defined in~\eqref{eq:carry}.  All 8 matrices $M_{a,b,g}$ are
precomputed once before the main loop.

% ── 4.2 Algorithm ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Computing $\pself(g)$ for All $g$ Simultaneously}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Compute $\pself(g)$ for all $g \in \{0,1\}^{16}$}
\label{alg:pself}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Truncated class parameters $(\alpha, \mu_\alpha, \beta, \mu_\beta)$
\Ensure Array $[\pself(g)]_{g=0}^{2^{16}-1}$

\State \textbf{Precompute} base matrices $M_{a,b,g} \in \mathbb{R}^{16 \times 16}$
       for $(a,b,g) \in \{0,1\}^3$ via~\eqref{eq:M8-def}

\State Initialise state matrix
       $L \in \mathbb{R}^{2^{16} \times 16}$ with $L[g, j] = \mathbf{1}[j = 5]$
       for all $g$ \Comment{carry state $e_5$: $(c_1,c_2,c_3,c_4)=(1,0,1,0)$}

\For{$i = 0$ \textbf{to} $15$}
  \State $a_{\mathrm{fix}} \gets (\alpha \ggg 7)_i$,\quad
         $\mathrm{fa} \gets (\mu_\alpha \ggg 7)_i$ \Comment{bit $i$ of rotated class}
  \State $b_{\mathrm{fix}} \gets \beta_i$,\quad
         $\mathrm{fb} \gets (\mu_\beta)_i$

  \For{$\hat{g} \in \{0, 1\}$} \Comment{one effective matrix per key-bit value}
    \State $M^{\mathrm{eff}}_i[\hat{g}] \gets \mathbf{0}_{16 \times 16}$
    \For{each free-bit combination $(a_i, b_i)$ (resp.\ fixed values)}
      \State $M^{\mathrm{eff}}_i[\hat{g}] \mathrel{+}=
             \tfrac{1}{2^{\mathrm{fa}+\mathrm{fb}}} \cdot M_{a_i, b_i, \hat{g}}$
    \EndFor
  \EndFor

  \State $g_{\mathrm{bit}} \gets \lfloor g / 2^i \rfloor \bmod 2$
         \quad for each $g \in [0, 2^{16})$

  \State $L[g \colon g_{\mathrm{bit}}=0] \mathrel{\gets}
         L[g \colon g_{\mathrm{bit}}=0]\cdot (M^{\mathrm{eff}}_i[0])^\top$ \Comment{row $\times$ matrix}
  \State $L[g \colon g_{\mathrm{bit}}=1] \mathrel{\gets}
         L[g \colon g_{\mathrm{bit}}=1]\cdot (M^{\mathrm{eff}}_i[1])^\top$
\EndFor

\Return $[\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{15} L[g,j]]_{g=0}^{2^{16}-1}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:pself} processes all $2^{16}$ values of $g$ in
parallel by maintaining the state distribution $L[g, \cdot]$ as a
$2^{16} \times 16$ matrix.  At each bit position $i$, the effective
matrix $M^{\mathrm{eff}}_i[\hat{g}]$ for each key-bit value
$\hat{g} \in \{0,1\}$ is formed by averaging $M_{a_i, b_i, \hat{g}}$
over the at most four combinations of free bits, then all rows of $L$
are multiplied by the matrix corresponding to the $i$-th bit of their
respective $g$.  After 16 such steps, each row of $L$ sums to
$\pself(g)$ by the product formula~\eqref{eq:pself}.

\subsection{Complexity}

\begin{proposition}
  \label{prop:complexity}
  Algorithm~\ref{alg:pself} computes $\pself(g)$ for all $g \in
  \{0,1\}^{16}$ in time $O(n \cdot 4 \cdot 2^n \cdot n^2)$ using
  $O(2^n \cdot n)$ memory, where $n = 16$.  For $n = 16$ this amounts
  to approximately $2^{28}$ floating-point operations and $2^{20}$
  memory words.
\end{proposition}

In practice the algorithm runs in under 0.3 seconds on a single CPU
core (Python/NumPy implementation), owing to the fully vectorised
matrix-multiply over the $2^{16}$ row dimension.

% ── 4.3 Instantiation ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Instantiation and Profile Shape}
\label{subsec:profile}

Applying Algorithm~\ref{alg:pself} to our truncated class
(Section~\ref{subsec:trunc}) yields the wrong-key profile
\[
  M_{\mathrm{BCT}}[g] = p_{\mathrm{rand}} + p_t \cdot \pself(g),
  \qquad
  S_{\mathrm{BCT}}[g] = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{M_{\mathrm{BCT}}[g]
                         \cdot (1 - M_{\mathrm{BCT}}[g])}},
\]
with $p_t = 2^{-11}$ and $p_{\mathrm{rand}} = 2^{-14}$.  Numerically:
$M_{\mathrm{BCT}}[0] \approx 2^{-11}$ (correct key), while
$M_{\mathrm{BCT}}[g] \approx p_{\mathrm{rand}} = 2^{-14}$ for
the overwhelming majority of wrong-key offsets $g$.  The profile
exhibits a sharply peaked spike at $g = 0$, with a smooth but steep
decay; this shape is the algebraic signature that the beam search
exploits.

% ============================================================
\section{Reconstructed Key-Recovery Attack}
\label{sec:attack}
% ============================================================

We now describe the full key-recovery attack instantiated with the
truncated differential distinguisher (Section~\ref{subsec:trunc})
and the algebraic WKP derived in Sections~\ref{sec:bct}
and~\ref{sec:algorithm}.

% ── 5.1 Sensitive-bit detection ─────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Truncated Differential Distinguisher Design: Sensitive-Bit Detection}
\label{subsec:sensitive}

The truncated class $\mathcal{T}$ must be chosen so that:
(i) $p_t \gg p_{\mathrm{rand}}$ (the truncated probability significantly
exceeds the random baseline), and (ii) $|\mathcal{T}|$ is large enough
to give a manageable data complexity.
We select the class by a \emph{sensitive-bit experiment} on $(r-1)$-round
\speck{}.

\paragraph{Procedure.}
Fix the target input difference
$\Delta_{\mathrm{in}} = (\mathtt{0x0040}, \mathtt{0x0000})$, which is
known to have substantial differential probability at reduced rounds of
\speck{}.
Generate $N_{\exp} = 2^{22}$ random plaintext pairs $(P_k, P_k \xor
\Delta_{\mathrm{in}})$ under uniformly random independent keys, encrypt
each pair through $r - 1$ rounds, and collect output differences
$\Delta^{(k)}_{\mathrm{out}} = (\Delta^{(k)}_L, \Delta^{(k)}_R)$.
For each bit position $j \in \{0, \ldots, 15\}$ of each word $w \in
\{L, R\}$, compute the \emph{empirical bias}
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:bias}
  \hat{\beta}_{w,j}
  \;=\;
  \left\lvert\,
    \frac{1}{N_{\exp}} \sum_{k=1}^{N_{\exp}} \Delta^{(k)}_{w,j}
    \;-\; \tfrac{1}{2}
  \right\rvert,
\end{equation}
where $\Delta^{(k)}_{w,j}$ denotes bit $j$ of word $w$ of the $k$-th
output difference.  A bit is declared \emph{sensitive} if
$\hat{\beta}_{w,j} > \theta$ for a chosen threshold $\theta > 0$;
otherwise it is \emph{free}.

\paragraph{Mask construction.}
Set $\mu_\alpha$ (resp.\ $\mu_\beta$) to have a $1$ at bit $j$ if and
only if bit $j$ of the left (resp.\ right) output word is free.  Set
the representative values $\alpha, \beta$ to the \emph{modal} (most
frequent) output difference in the sensitive positions.  The resulting
masks for our instantiation are:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:masks}
  \alpha = \mathtt{0x0109},\quad
  \mu_\alpha = \mathtt{0x6A74},\quad
  \beta = \mathtt{0x8003},\quad
  \mu_\beta = \mathtt{0x6AFC},
\end{equation}
giving $n_{\mathrm{free}} = 18$ free bits and $n_{\mathrm{fixed}} = 14$
sensitive bits.  The measured truncated differential probability is
$p_t = 2^{-11}$, providing an SNR of $p_t / p_{\mathrm{rand}} = 2^3 = 8$
over the random baseline $p_{\mathrm{rand}} = 2^{-14}$.

% ── 5.2 Data generation ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Data Generation}
\label{subsec:datagen}

The attack requires $S$ independent \emph{structures}, each comprising
$N$ ciphertext pairs $(C^{(s,i)}, C'^{(s,i)})$ for $s \in [S]$,
$i \in [N]$.  Within each structure, pairs share the same unknown key
$k$ but use independently random base plaintexts.  Pairs are
generated as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Draw $N$ random plaintexts $P^{(i)} \in \mathbb{F}_2^{32}$.
  \item Apply a fixed-key one-round decryption (with dummy key $0$) to
    both $P^{(i)}$ and $P^{(i)} \xor \Delta_{\mathrm{in}}$ to obtain
    extended plaintexts
    $\hat{P}^{(i)} = F_0^{-1}(P^{(i)})$ and
    $\hat{P}'^{(i)} = F_0^{-1}(P^{(i)} \xor \Delta_{\mathrm{in}})$;
    this prepends an additional unkeyed round so that the distinguisher
    operates over the correct (one-extended) internal difference.
  \item Encrypt: $C^{(s,i)} = E_k^{(r)}(\hat{P}^{(i)})$,\;
    $C'^{(s,i)} = E_k^{(r)}(\hat{P}'^{(i)})$.
\end{enumerate}

Total data: $S \cdot N$ ciphertext pairs per trial.  In our experiments
we use $S = 32$ structures and $N = 2^{14}$ pairs per structure,
totalling $2^{19}$ ciphertext pairs per trial.

% ── 5.3 Full attack algorithm ────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Two-Layer Bayesian Beam Search with UCB Structure Selection}
\label{subsec:attack-alg}

The full attack is split across two algorithms.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:attack} covers phases~(1) and~(2): a UCB-guided
outer loop selects ciphertext structures and runs a two-layer beam
search to simultaneously recover the last two round subkeys
$k_{r-1}$ and $k_{r-2}$.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:verifier} covers phase~(3): a verifier exhaustively
explores the Hamming-$\le 2$ neighbourhood of the best candidate pair
to correct small errors.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{UCB Two-Layer Beam Search (Phase 1--2)}
\label{alg:attack}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Ciphertext structures $\{(C^{(s,i)}, C'^{(s,i)})\}$,
         WKP arrays $M_{\mathrm{BCT}}$, $S_{\mathrm{BCT}}$,
         beam width $B$, iterations $T$,
         thresholds $\tau_1, \tau_2$, UCB constant $\alpha$
\Ensure Best candidate $(\hat{k}_1, \hat{k}_2)$ and pod index $s^{\dag}$

\State $v^* \gets -\infty$;\quad
       $(\hat{k}_1, \hat{k}_2) \gets (0, 0)$;\quad
       $\ell_{\mathrm{best}}[s] \gets -\infty$,\;
       $\nu[s] \gets \varepsilon$ \quad for all $s$

\For{$j = 1$ \textbf{to} $T$}
  \If{$v^* > \tau_2$} \textbf{break} \EndIf
  \State $s^* \!\gets\! \arg\max_{s}\!\bigl[\ell_{\mathrm{best}}[s]
         + \alpha\sqrt{{\log_2 j}/{\nu[s]}}\bigr]$;\quad
         $\nu[s^*] \mathrel{+}= 1$

  \Statex \textit{// Layer 1: recover $k_{r-1}$}
  \State $\mathcal{K}_1 \gets B$ random values in $\{0,1\}^{14}$
  \For{$\ell = 1$ \textbf{to} $5$}
    \For{each $k' \in \mathcal{K}_1$}
      \State $h(k') \gets \#\{i : F_{k'}^{-1}(C^{(s^*,i)}) \xor
             F_{k'}^{-1}(C'^{(s^*,i)}) \in \mathcal{T}\}$
      \State $\hat{r}(k') \gets h(k')/N$;\quad
             $v(k') \gets \log_2(h(k') \cdot D)$
    \EndFor
    \State $\sigma(b) \gets \bigl\lVert[\hat{r}(k'_j) -
           M[b \xor k'_j]] \cdot S[b \xor k'_j]\bigr\rVert_2$
           \;for each $b$
    \State $\mathcal{K}_1 \gets$ top-$B$ by $-\sigma$ with random
           2-bit extension
  \EndFor
  \State $\ell_{\mathrm{best}}[s^*] \gets
         \max(\ell_{\mathrm{best}}[s^*],\, \max_{k'} v(k'))$

  \Statex \textit{// Layer 2: recover $k_{r-2}$ given $k_{r-1}$}
  \If{$\max_{k'} v(k') > \tau_1$}
    \For{each $k_1 \in \mathcal{K}_1$ with $v(k_1) > \tau_1$}
      \State $(\mathcal{K}_2, v_2) \gets \mathrm{BeamSearch}(
             \{F_{k_1}^{-1}(C^{(s^*,\cdot)})\},\, B)$
      \If{$\max v_2 > v^*$}
        \State $v^* \gets \max v_2$;\quad
               $(\hat{k}_1, \hat{k}_2) \gets
               (k_1,\, \arg\max v_2)$;\quad
               $s^{\dag} \gets s^*$
      \EndIf
    \EndFor
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\Return $(\hat{k}_1, \hat{k}_2, s^{\dag})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Verifier: Hamming-$\le 2$ Neighbourhood Refinement (Phase 3)}
\label{alg:verifier}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Best candidate $(\hat{k}_1, \hat{k}_2)$, pod $s^{\dag}$,
         verifier pairs $N_{\mathrm{ver}} = 128$
\Ensure Refined subkey pair $(\hat{k}_{r-1}, \hat{k}_{r-2})$

\State $\mathcal{N} \gets \{w \in \{0,1\}^{16} : \mathrm{wt}(w) \le 2\}$
       \quad ($|\mathcal{N}| = 137$)
\Repeat
  \State $(e_1^*, e_2^*) \gets \arg\max_{(e_1,e_2)\in\mathcal{N}^2}
         \log_2\!\bigl(\#\{i \le N_{\mathrm{ver}} :
         F_{\hat{k}_1 \xor e_1}^{-1}(C^{(s^{\dag},i)}) \xor
         F_{\hat{k}_1 \xor e_1}^{-1}(C'^{(s^{\dag},i)}) \in
         \mathcal{T}\} \cdot D\bigr)$
         \Comment{over two-round peel}
  \State $(\hat{k}_1, \hat{k}_2) \gets
         (\hat{k}_1 \xor e_1^*,\; \hat{k}_2 \xor e_2^*)$
\Until{no improvement}
\Return $(\hat{k}_1, \hat{k}_2)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Scoring details.}
Within the beam, each candidate subkey $k'$ is scored on two complementary
scales.  The \emph{volume score} $v(k') = \log_2(h(k') \cdot D)$, where
$D = 1 / (p_t \cdot 2^{-5}) = 2^{16}$, compares the raw hit count to
the expected correct-key count.  Under the correct key offset $g = 0$,
\[
  \mathbb{E}[v \mid g = 0] \;=\; \log_2(N \cdot p_t \cdot D) \;=\;
  \log_2(2^{14} \cdot 2^{-11} \cdot 2^{16}) \;=\; 19,
\]
while under noise, $\mathbb{E}[v \mid g \ne 0] \approx 16$.  The
thresholds $\tau_1 = 18$ and $\tau_2 = 22$ bracket these values,
triggering layer-2 entry and early exit respectively.  The
\emph{Bayesian residual score} $\sigma(b)$ (line~14) additionally
ranks the pool using the full WKP shape, selecting candidates whose
empirical hit-rate profile best matches the algebraic prediction
$M_{\mathrm{BCT}}$.

\paragraph{UCB exploration.}
The UCB priority (line~6) assigns higher priority to structures with
high peak scores and to structures that have been visited infrequently.
The constant $\alpha = \sqrt{N}$ controls the exploration-exploitation
trade-off, following the multi-armed bandit analysis of~\cite{ACF02}.

\paragraph{Verifier.}
The verifier loop (lines~25--28) exhaustively scores all $137^2 \approx
1.9 \times 10^4$ candidate pairs in the Hamming-$\le 2$ neighbourhood
of the current best guess, using a fixed sample of $N_{\mathrm{ver}} = 128$
pairs from the best-scoring structure.  Each candidate requires only
two one-round decryptions and a truncated hit check, so the
neighbourhood scan costs $O(137^2 \cdot N_{\mathrm{ver}})$ operations.

\section{Complexity Analysis}
\label{sec:complexity}

We analyse the data, time, and memory complexity of the truncated-BCT
key-recovery attack on $r$-round \speck{}, following the framework of
Bao et al.~\cite{BLY23}.  Throughout this section we use the 7-round
parameter set from Table~\ref{tab:params}.

% ── 6.1 Data Complexity ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Data Complexity}

The theoretical data complexity is $2 \cdot S \cdot N$ chosen plaintexts
(the factor 2 counts both elements of each ciphertext pair):
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:data}
  \mathcal{D} \;=\; 2 \cdot S \cdot N \;=\; 2 \cdot 32 \cdot 2^{14} \;=\; 2^{20}.
\end{equation}
This is the \emph{worst-case} data requirement; in practice early termination
(when $v^*$ exceeds $\tau_2 = 22$ before all $T = 300$ UCB iterations are
consumed) reduces actual data usage substantially.

\paragraph{Lower bound on $N$.}
For the beam search to succeed on a given structure, the correct
subkey's volume score must exceed the layer-1 threshold $\tau_1 = 18$,
i.e.\ the hit count must satisfy
\[
  h(k_{r-1}) \;\ge\; \frac{2^{\tau_1}}{D}
  \;=\; \frac{2^{18}}{2^{16}} \;=\; 4.
\]
Under the correct key, $h \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(N, p_t)$ with mean
$N p_t$.  For $N = 2^{14}$:
\[
  \mathbb{E}[h] = 2^{14} \cdot 2^{-11} = 8, \qquad
  \mathrm{Var}[h] \approx 8.
\]
By a Poisson approximation, $\Pr[h \ge 4] = 1 - \Pr[\mathrm{Pois}(8) \le 3]
\approx 1 - 0.0424 > 0.95$, so a single structure of $N = 2^{14}$ pairs
already exceeds the threshold with probability $\ge 95\%$.  The minimum
data complexity for a single-trial attack is therefore approximately
$2N = 2^{15}$ plaintexts (one structure); the use of $S = 32$ structures
provides redundancy and accelerates convergence of the UCB selector.

\paragraph{Effect of the SNR.}
The $3$-bit gap $\mathbb{E}[v \mid g{=}0] - \mathbb{E}[v \mid g{\ne}0] = 3$
(Section~\ref{subsec:datagen}) ensures that the correct subkey consistently
ranks well above the noise floor.  Tightening the truncated class (more
fixed bits) would increase $p_t/p_{\mathrm{rand}}$ and reduce data
requirements but raises the risk that the truncated differential itself
becomes too rare to detect; the chosen $n_{\mathrm{fixed}} = 14$ balances
these two effects.

% ── 6.2 Time Complexity ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Time Complexity}
\label{subsec:time}

Following Bao et al.~\cite{BLY23}, we normalise time in units of
equivalent $r$-round \speck{} encryptions.
Let $\mathcal{E}$ denote the cost of one $r$-round encryption.
A single one-round decryption (the elementary operation in the beam
search) costs $\mathcal{E}/r$.

\paragraph{Online phase.}

The dominant costs per UCB iteration are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Layer-1 beam} ($5$ inner iterations, $B = 32$ candidates,
    $N = 2^{14}$ pairs): $5 \cdot B \cdot N$ one-round decryptions
    $= 5 \cdot 32 \cdot 2^{14} = 5 \cdot 2^{19}$ round operations.
  \item \textbf{Bayesian residual} (\texttt{bayesian\_rank\_kr}):
    for each inner iteration, an $\mathbb{R}^{B \times 2^{14}}$ matrix-norm
    computation costing $O(B \cdot 2^{14})$ multiply-adds
    $= 32 \cdot 2^{14} = 2^{19}$ operations per inner iteration.
  \item \textbf{Layer-2 beam} (triggered $\sim T_2$ times per trial,
    each of identical cost to one layer-1 beam call):
    $T_2 \cdot 5 \cdot B \cdot N$ round operations, where $T_2 \le T$.
\end{enumerate}

Over $T = 300$ outer UCB iterations with at most $T_2 \le T = 300$
layer-2 calls (in practice $T_2 \ll T$ since layer-2 is only triggered when
a layer-1 beam exceeds $\tau_1 = 18$):
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:time-online}
  C_{\mathrm{online}}
  \;\approx\;
  (T + T_2) \cdot 5 \cdot B \cdot N \cdot \frac{\mathcal{E}}{r}
  \;\approx\;
  300 \cdot 5 \cdot 32 \cdot 2^{14} \cdot \frac{\mathcal{E}}{7}
  \;\approx\; 2^{29} \cdot \mathcal{E},
\end{equation}
where we used $r = 7$ and approximated $T + T_2 \approx 2T$.

\paragraph{Verifier phase.}

The verifier exhausts $|\mathcal{N}|^2 \approx 137^2 \approx 2^{14.1}$
two-round-peel candidates over $N_{\mathrm{ver}} = 128 = 2^7$ pairs:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:time-verify}
  C_{\mathrm{ver}} \;\approx\; 2^{14.1} \cdot 2 \cdot 2^7 \cdot \frac{\mathcal{E}}{7}
  \;\approx\; 2^{20} \cdot \mathcal{E}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Offline phase.}

The BCT profile computation (Algorithm~\ref{alg:pself}) runs in
$O(16 \cdot 16^2 \cdot 2^{16})$ floating-point operations, roughly
$2^{28}$ scalar operations, and is done once before any attack trial.
Normalised: $\approx 2^{28} / (r \cdot C_\mathrm{mult}) \mathcal{E}$,
where $C_\mathrm{mult}$ is the number of scalar multiplications per
round, which is negligible relative to the online cost.

\paragraph{Total time complexity.}

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:time-total}
  \mathcal{T} \;\approx\; C_{\mathrm{online}} + C_{\mathrm{ver}}
  \;\approx\; 2^{29} \cdot \mathcal{E}.
\end{equation}

The exact constant depends on the ratio of decryption-round throughput to
floating-point throughput on the target platform; an experimentally
calibrated value is provided in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\paragraph{Advantage.}

The 64-bit master key of \speck{} spans a $2^{64}$ search space.  After
recovering the two last-round subkeys $(k_{r-1}, k_{r-2})$, the remaining
master-key bits can be determined from the key schedule in negligible time.
The advantage over brute force is approximately
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:advantage}
  \mathrm{Adv}
  \;=\; \log_2(2^{64}) - \log_2(\mathcal{T} / \mathcal{E})
  \;\approx\; 64 - 29 \;=\; 35 \text{ bits}.
\end{equation}

% ── 6.3 Memory Complexity ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────

% ── 6.5 Extension to 14-Round Speck32/64 ──────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Theoretical Extension to 14-Round \speck{}}
\label{subsec:14round}

We derive the theoretical complexity of a 14-round key-recovery
attack obtained by extending the framework from two beam-search layers
to three, using the \emph{1+9+3+1} round decomposition:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{1 backward extension}: apply one round of
    \speck{} decryption (with the unknown subkey set to zero) to each
    plaintext before the encrypt-and-distinguish phase, effectively
    extending the distinguisher depth by one round for free.
  \item \textbf{9 core rounds}: encrypt with the full 14-round
    oracle; the truncated-BCT score is computed from the ciphertext
    pairs after this step.
  \item \textbf{3 UCB beam-search layers}: recover subkeys
    $k_{13},k_{12},k_{11}$ by three sequential beam-search layers,
    each peeling one round off the top of the ciphertext.
  \item \textbf{1 verifier round}: confirm $(k_{11},k_{12},k_{13})$
    and recover $k_{10}$ via a joint Hamming-$\le 2$ neighbourhood
    search.
\end{itemize}
Total round coverage: $1+9+3+1 = 14$.

The BCT offline phase (Algorithm~\ref{alg:pself}) is
\emph{round-agnostic}: it depends only on the truncated class
$\mathcal{T}$ and is unchanged when the round count varies.

\paragraph{Prerequisite: 9-round truncated differential.}

Let $\mathcal{T}$ be a truncated class with $n_\mathrm{fixed}$
fixed output-difference bits (so $p_\mathrm{rand} = 2^{-n_\mathrm{fixed}}$),
and let
\[
  p_t^{(9)} \;=\; 2^{-\rho}, \qquad 0 < \rho < n_\mathrm{fixed},
\]
be the 9-round truncated differential probability over the core rounds.
The attack is viable iff $p_t^{(9)} > p_\mathrm{rand}$, i.e.\
$\rho < n_\mathrm{fixed}$.  The SNR at the layer-1 beam step is
\[
  \mathrm{SNR} \;=\;
  \frac{p_t^{(9)}}{p_\mathrm{rand}} \;=\; 2^{n_\mathrm{fixed} - \rho}.
\]

\paragraph{Data complexity.}

Each structure contributes $N$ ciphertext pairs; the correct subkey at
layer~1 accumulates $\mathbb{E}[h \mid g{=}0] = N \cdot p_t^{(9)} = N
\cdot 2^{-\rho}$ hits on average.  Setting $N = 2^{\rho+3}$ gives
$\mathbb{E}[h] = 8$, matching the comfortable margin of the 7-round
attack.  With $S = 32$ structures the total data requirement is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:data14}
  \mathcal{D}_{14} \;=\; 2SN \;=\; 2^{\rho+9} \text{ chosen plaintexts.}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Online time: three-layer beam search.}

Layer 2 is triggered whenever layer 1 succeeds; layer 3 whenever layer 2
succeeds.  In the conservative worst case all three are triggered at every
UCB iteration ($T_1=T_2=T_3=T$):
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:time14-online}
  C_\mathrm{online}^{(14)}
  \;\approx\;
  3T \cdot 5BN \cdot \tfrac{\mathcal{E}}{14}
  \;\approx\;
  3 \cdot 300 \cdot 5 \cdot 32 \cdot 2^{\rho+3} \cdot \tfrac{\mathcal{E}}{14}
  \;\approx\;
  2^{\rho+19.2} \cdot \mathcal{E}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Verifier time.}

The verifier jointly searches the Hamming-$\le 2$ neighbourhood of the
beam output for all four last-round subkeys
$(k_{13},k_{12},k_{11},k_{10})$.
For a 16-bit word, $|\mathcal{N}_{\le2}| = 1+16+\binom{16}{2} = 137$,
so the candidate count is $137^4 \approx 2^{28.4}$.  Each candidate
requires four-round decryption over $N_\mathrm{ver}=128$ pairs:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:time14-verify}
  C_\mathrm{ver}^{(14)}
  \;\approx\;
  2^{28.4} \cdot 2^7 \cdot \tfrac{4}{14} \cdot \mathcal{E}
  \;\approx\; 2^{33.6} \cdot \mathcal{E}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Total complexity and advantage.}

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:time14-total}
  \mathcal{T}_{14}
  \;\approx\;
  \max\!\bigl(2^{\rho+19.2},\,2^{33.6}\bigr) \cdot \mathcal{E}.
\end{equation}
The verifier dominates whenever $\rho \le 14.4$.  For any
$\rho \le 14$ (i.e.\ any viable truncated differential with
$p_t^{(9)} > p_\mathrm{rand}$) the advantage is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:adv14}
  \mathrm{Adv}_{14}
  \;\approx\; 64 - 33.6 \;=\; 30.4 \text{ bits.}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Open problem: 9-round truncated differential search.}

The only missing ingredient is a truncated class $\mathcal{T}$ with
$p_t^{(9)} > p_\mathrm{rand}$ for \speck{}.  Existing MILP-
and SAT-based trail search methods~\cite{ALLW14} apply directly and
can enumerate truncated trails at depth~9; we leave this search to
future work.  All other components of the framework---the BCT
profile, the UCB beam search, and the verifier---extend to 14 rounds
without modification.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Implementation}

All experiments are implemented in Python~3.11 using NumPy~1.26 for
vectorised arithmetic.  No GPU or specialised hardware is used; all
runs execute on a single CPU core.  The BCT-algebraic WKP computation
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:pself}) is implemented as a vectorised $65536 \times
16$ state-matrix propagation and processes all $2^{16}$ key offsets in a
single pass.

Source code is available at the project repository alongside the
experimental scripts.

\subsection{BCT Wrong-Key Profile Verification}

We first verify the correctness of the algebraic WKP before running the
key-recovery experiment.

\paragraph{Offline pre-computation.}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:pself} was applied to the truncated class
$\mathcal{T}$ defined in Section~\ref{subsec:trunc}
($A=\mathtt{0x0109}$, $A_M=\mathtt{0x6A74}$; $B=\mathtt{0x8003}$,
$B_M=\mathtt{0x6AFC}$) over all $2^{16}$ key offsets $g$.  The
computation completed in $0.2$\,s.  The resulting profile satisfies

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\pself(0) = 1.000$ (the class is closed under the correct key,
        as required by Proposition~\ref{prop:pself0});
  \item $\overline{\pself} = \mathbb{E}_{g \ne 0}[\pself(g)] \approx
        3.80 \times 10^{-3}$;
  \item $\pself(g) = 0$ for the majority of wrong-key offsets $g$.
\end{itemize}

The corresponding WKP means satisfy
$M_{\mathrm{BCT}}[0] = P_{\mathrm{RAND}} + p_t = 5.49 \times 10^{-4}$
(correct key) and
$\overline{M_{\mathrm{BCT}}} \approx 6.29 \times 10^{-5}$ (average wrong
key), giving a signal-to-noise ratio of $M_{\mathrm{BCT}}[0] /
\overline{M_{\mathrm{BCT}}} \approx 8.7 = 2^{3.1}$.

$\tau_1 = 18$.  This validates the BCT-algebraic WKP as an accurate
predictive model for the score distribution.

\paragraph{Calibration against Speck throughput.}

We measure the native Speck32/64 NumPy vectorised throughput at
approximately $2^{26.8}$ round-function calls per second on a single CPU

$T = 300$ iterations, the predicted wall time per trial is
$300 \times 0.5 \approx 150$\,s in the worst case, consistent with
the measured average.

\paragraph{Comparison with success rate at lower rounds.}

The 6-round \speck{} version of the same attack (using identical parameters
but $r = 6$) succeeds with higher probability, as the signal is stronger:
$P_T$ increases and the verifier phase operates over a shorter key schedule.
We tested 20 trials at 6 rounds and observed a success rate exceeding
$90\%$, consistent with the wider SNR gap in that setting.

\subsection{Summary}

The experimental results confirm the three main theoretical predictions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The algebraic WKP correctly identifies $g = 0$ (correct key offset)
        as the unique mode of $M_{\mathrm{BCT}}$, with $\pself(0) = 1$.
  \item The expected log-score $\mathbb{E}[v \mid k^*] = 19$ accurately
        describes the observed score for the correct candidate.
  \item The two-layer UCB beam search recovers both last-round subkeys
        using $2^{19}$ chosen-plaintext pairs with no neural-network component.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented a fully classical, algebraically grounded replacement
for Gohr's neural-network-based key recovery on round-reduced \speck{}.
The core novelty is a \emph{closed-form BCT-algebraic formula} for the
wrong-key profile $\pself(g)$ of a truncated differential distinguisher,
derived from a 16-state transfer-matrix propagation over the carry
structure of modular addition.  This allows the Bayesian key-recovery
framework to be deployed without any neural-network training, empirical
sampling of the WKP, or distinguisher evaluation at test time; the entire
statistical model is pre-computed analytically in $0.2$\,s.

\paragraph{Key contributions.}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Truncated self-returning BCT probability.}
    We formalise the notion of a \emph{self-returning} truncated
    differential class and derive an exact BCT-style formula for
    $\pself(g)$ as the probability that a difference in class
    $\mathcal{T}$ remains in $\mathcal{T}$ after one round of \speck{}
    under a key shift by $g$.  The formula factors over the 16 bit
    positions via a transfer-matrix product, giving an $O(16 \cdot 16^2
    \cdot 2^{16})$ algorithm (Algorithm~\ref{alg:pself}) that runs in
    $0.2$\,s for all $2^{16}$ offsets simultaneously.

  \item \textbf{Algebraic wrong-key profile.}
    The WKP $M_{\mathrm{BCT}}[g] = P_{\mathrm{RAND}} + p_t \cdot
    \pself(g)$ is derived analytically with no sampling.  Its dominant
    feature — a sharp spike at $g = 0$ — is a direct consequence of the
    class-closure lemma (Proposition~\ref{prop:pself0}) and the low density of
    self-returning classes among all possible $g$.

  \item \textbf{Fully classical key recovery on 7-round \speck{}.}
    The two-layer UCB beam search (Algorithms~\ref{alg:attack}--\ref{alg:verifier}) recovers
    both last-round subkeys of 7-round \speck{} from $2^{19}$ chosen
    plaintexts with a success rate exceeding $70\%$ and a time complexity
    of approximately $2^{29}$ round-equivalent operations, achieving a
    35-bit advantage over brute force.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Relation to neural distinguishers.}

Our results demonstrate that the statistical structure underlying Gohr's
attack — the correlation between correct and wrong key offsets manifested
in the hit-rate distribution — is fully captured by classical differential
tools without any learning.  The connection to the BCT provides a
theoretical explanation for why the key-ranking score in Gohr's framework
is so accurate: the neural network implicitly learns the same BCT-self-returning
probability that we compute algebraically.  This is consistent with the
interpretability analysis of Benamira et al.~\cite{BGMR23} and the
wrong-key randomisation study of Bao et al.~\cite{BLY23}, and provides a
rigorous analytic counterpart to both.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.}

The current attack targets 7 rounds of \speck{}; Gohr's neural approach
reaches 11 rounds.  Extending our framework to deeper distinguishers
requires multi-round truncated differential trails and transfer matrices
that track carry correlations across several additions, which grows
combinatorially.  A promising direction is to use the single-round BCT
formula as a building block in an iterated transfer-matrix product,
analogous to the meet-in-the-middle differentials used in
\cite{ALLW14,BLY23}.

A second direction is to apply the same algebraic WKP methodology to
other ARX ciphers (Simon, Speck variants, LEA) or to the boomerang
setting, where the BCT already provides a natural framework for
computing wrong-key hit rates.

Finally, we note that the connection between the BCT and the log-likelihood
ratio used in Bayesian key ranking (Equation~\eqref{eq:gohr-score}) suggests a
more general framework in which any algebraically computable wrong-key
distribution can serve as a drop-in replacement for the neural
distinguisher — potentially enabling fully theory-driven attacks in
settings where neural-network training data is scarce or the cipher
structure admits a closed-form analysis.
